This report examines the reality of SaaS data loss caused by four common 
scenarios through relevant, real-world examples alongside realistic (yet hypo-
thetical) situations and asks a practical question: When there’s a disruption to 
production, who’s in control of the data and how is business continuity ensured? 
The four Ms of data loss show how control is lost, while immutable, independent 
backup plus practiced recovery, sound data governance, and considered data 
sovereignty show how control is maintained or regained.
One thing is clear: Cyber resilience in the SaaS era is not a one-time restore 
exercise. Instead it needs to be a broader strategy shaped by how data is lost, 
how recovery is tested, how backup data is stored, how readiness matures, and 
whether control remains under the sovereignty and dependency conditions an 
organization faces, both from inside and external compliance demands.

The SaaS data protection gap
Responsibility in the disaster recovery equation
Cloud adoption made a lot of things easier, but it also introduced the shared responsibility model that every 
SaaS provider champions. One of the biggest changes is that application availability as well as data avail-
ability and recoverability are no longer owned by the same entity, leading to a data protection gap stemming 
from a common misunderstanding of what is protected and by whom.
Along with these changes to application and data responsibilities, cybercriminals are targeting these cloud 
services aggressively. Compliance requirements keep rising, including the need for measured data sover-
eignty, which has moved from a legal edge case to a board-level resilience question. On top of all of this, AI 
is speeding up both administration and data creation, as well as increasing the magnitude of mistakes and 
lowering the bar of entry for cybercriminals.
Most organizations already have some form of business continuity or disaster recovery plan, but the data 
protection gap remains as many of those plans treat SaaS data as if it’s automatically protected (it’s not) 
by the application provider through native backup offerings. The shared responsibility model applies, 
which tells us: The SaaS provider runs the service, but the customer remains responsible for data, identities, 
configurations, access, and recoverability (as well as for the risks and compliance requirements placed on 
that data).
Figure 1. The shared responsibility model highlights the user’s responsibilities for data, identity, and more.
A recent CIO MarketPulse white paper highlights this gap: More than one third of surveyed organizations still 
rely entirely on native SaaS protections. Nearly half have already suffered a major data loss event, and 37% 
still do nothing beyond native protection. Confidence also runs ahead of readiness: 53% of respondents 
believe they could recover from a major data loss within 24 hours, yet 11% say it would take a month or more 
— or that they may not be able to fully recover at all.

The SaaS data protection gap

Keeping control
What cyber resilience looks like in a SaaS disruption
In an on-premises model, meeting a recovery time objective is largely about recovering systems and the data 
they store. The organization owns both layers, so the recovery model is more unified. In SaaS, the provider 
operates the application, but the customer still carries the consequences of lost, corrupted, inaccessible, 
or legally constrained data. That means resilience has to be understood in terms of control: control over 
accessible backup copies, control over identity and access management, control over data sovereignty and 
governance, and control over the environment and obligations that shape recovery.
Access during disruption
How completely and quickly can critical 
data be accessed, even if the SaaS appli-
cation, tenant, or control place is unhealthy 
or down?
Restore after recovery
How completely and quickly can that data 
be restored once the application tenant 
is operational again — and in the order the 
business actually needs?
That split is easy to overlook because SaaS vendors understandably talk about uptime and service resil-
ience. But availability of the service is not the same as recoverability of the customer’s data. Shared respon-
sibility does not end at service availability, but rather it defines who owns the risk. The provider is responsible 
for running the platform; the customer is still responsible for ensuring that business-critical data, identity 
structures, and recovery paths remain protected, independent, and usable when things go wrong.
The key takeaway
You can outsource operations, but you can’t outsource accountability.
Ransomware is the clearest example of why this matters. Modern attacks are designed to do more than 
encrypt files. They try to break trust in production data, disable recovery options, and force a business into a 
time-compressed decision. Ransomware-as-a-service lowered the barrier to entry. Attackers increasingly 
target backups because weakening the recovery path is often faster than negotiating. That’s why resilience 
depends on more than “having copies.” Those copies need meaningful separation, logical air-gapping, 
immutability, and a recovery path the attacker cannot easily touch.
Data governance and data sovereignty add the next layer. Governance defines what must be protected, 
retained, restored, and reviewed, while sovereignty defines which laws, regulations, compliance obliga-
tions, providers, and jurisdictions shape that protection. Together with shared responsibility, they go a long 
way toward determining whether an organization truly controls its SaaS data or not. In practice, that control 
is achieved through immutable, independent, air-gapped backup and tested recovery. This is the context in 
which the four Ms framing becomes useful.

Data loss rarely happens as a single, random event. In practice, most incidents follow recognizable patterns 
that make them easier to plan for than they first appear. One useful way to frame those patterns is through 
the four Ms of data loss: malicious attacks, mistakes by admins, mishaps at the cloud provider, and migra-
tions gone bad. Each category shows a different way control can be lost, whether through attack, error, 
dependency, or change.
This framework matters because it moves the conversation from abstract risk to operational control. The 
causes differ, but the business question is usually the same: Can the organization identify a trusted recovery 
point, restore the right data quickly enough to limit disruption, and do so under conditions it can trust and 
control? Verizon’s 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report reinforces why that framing holds up. Third-party 
involvement in breaches doubled to 30%, exploitation of vulnerabilities rose 34%, and credential abuse 
remained one of the most common initial access patterns. Some disruptions are malicious, some are struc-
tural, and many begin somewhere in between.
Not every incident is a cyberattack. Some begin with a bad retention change, an integration failure, or a 
provider-side event that makes the control plane unreliable. The common thread is that production can stop 
being the sole source of truth. Resilience then depends on regaining control through independent backup, 
practiced recovery, good governance, and sovereignty-minded design.

Malicious attacks
Malicious attacks are among the most disruptive causes of data loss because they are designed to compro-
mise both the production environment and the organization’s ability to recover from it. Ransomware, 
credential abuse, destructive compromise, and malicious deletion all aim at the same outcome: break trust 
in production, degrade access, and turn time into leverage. In SaaS environments, that often means the 
recovery sequence matters just as much as the backup itself. You may need to restore identities before data, 
re-establish permissions before access, and verify that the tenant is safe before pushing content back into it.
A published INNOVISION case shows why that sequencing matters. The customer had recently deployed 
independent backup for Microsoft Entra ID and Microsoft 365 after recognizing that cloud workloads still 
needed their own protection. Soon after, the environment was hit by a targeted cyberattack. Because the 
recovery path was already in place, the organization was able to restore all impacted Entra ID user accounts 
and associated Microsoft 365 data with a recovery time of less than three hours. Restoring the compromised 
identities first avoided a slower, manual rebuild in which user accounts would have needed to be recreated 
one by one, complete with undocumented settings, permissions, and dependencies. That’s an important 
SaaS lesson: In a cloud incident, restoring content without restoring the identity layer can still leave the busi-
ness operationally stuck.

The same INNOVISION recovery story also shows that backup can matter after the restore, not just during 
it. While it is the speed of recovery that’s essential during, it is completeness of recovery after that matters. 
During the post-incident investigation and cyber-insurance process, the customer needed evidence of 
what had changed in Entra ID objects and when. Metadata differencing was used to trace attribute-level 
changes on privileged objects and provide technical evidence for the postmortem. That expands the role 
of backup from restoration into investigation, documentation, and incident validation, and makes it a critical 
part for being able to secure cyber insurance.
The Technology Did It case makes a different point: the integrity of the backup path itself. In that case, a U.S. 
government supplier was hit by Conti ransomware roughly three months after onboarding. According to the 
published account, the protected data remained unavailable to the attacker, the organization restored its 
data back to SharePoint, all data was restored within 30 minutes, and the business was back to normal by 
Monday morning after a Friday attack. The headline is speed, but the deeper point is separation. Recovery 
was fast because the recovery copies were still trustworthy, immutable to the impacts of ransomware.

A third example from an SMB customer organization in education is useful for a quieter reason. The attack 
affected the organization’s Microsoft 365 environment while backups were still running, but the business 
still had access to a last-known-good snapshot from before the corruption spread. That gave the team a 
clean point in time to recover from rather than forcing it to trust the production environment after the fact.

data still exists somewhere — it’s whether a clean, usable recovery point exists outside the compromised

The four Ms of data loss: Malicious attacks

Mistakes by admins
Administrative mistakes are a common source of SaaS data loss because they happen with legitimate 
access, often in live production, and frequently before anyone recognizes the downstream impact. In SaaS 
environments, the blast radius can extend well beyond the obvious deleted object. A rushed retention 
change can alter legal-hold behavior. A policy edit can’t be undone. A deleted identity object can break 
application access, automation, and approvals in one move.
One customer example involving a large retail chain specializing in home furnishing, makes the point clearly. 
A retention setting in the tenant was accidentally changed from five years to one year, and the result was 
not a minor correction but the need to restore several thousand mailboxes. The lesson is not only that policy 
mistakes are easy to make, but that recovering from them often requires more than bringing back message 
content. Mailbox structure, metadata, state, and context still have to be usable after the restore. In poli-
cy-driven environments like Microsoft 365, a single admin action can therefore become a large operational 
recovery event.

Identity mistakes can be even more punishing. Consider accidental deletion of a security group, app registra-
tion, or enterprise application. If the deleted object controls access to critical business tools, employees can 
lose access immediately, integrations can fail, and permissions may have to be rebuilt manually. Microsoft’s 
own recovery guidance on restoring app registrations and restoring soft-deleted enterprise applications 
makes clear that native recovery exists only for a limited period. After 30 days, those objects are permanent-
ly deleted. Even inside the recovery window, restoring the object alone is not always enough. Relationships 
— memberships, owners, assignments, connected links — are often what make the object usable in the first 
place. That’s why identity-aware recovery, including cross-tenant restore, matters in practice.

The four Ms of data loss: Mistakes by admins

AI adds a new twist to this category, but not a new principle. In a recent TechCrunch example, a Meta AI 
security researcher asked an OpenClaw agent to review an overstuffed inbox and suggest what to delete 
or archive. Instead, the agent began deleting emails outright and ignored attempts to stop it from a phone, 
forcing direct intervention on the device running the tool. Publicly, that reads as an AI-agent mishap. Opera-
tionally, it’s an administrative error amplified by automation: destructive authority was granted, live data was 
used, and the controls around scale and approval were weaker than the task required. The mistake was not 
just the click, but the trust model around the automation.
That’s the broader lesson for this category. “Mistakes by admins” doesn’t just mean someone pressed the 
wrong button, it includes excessive permissions, rushed production changes, poorly scoped automa-
tion, and the move from test data to live data without adequate safeguards. In these scenarios, recovery 
depends on having versioned, external backups that preserve not only content but also metadata, object 
relationships, and identity structures.
How control is regained
•	Narrow destructive authority
•	Protect policy states and identity objects
•	Make sure backup preserves relationships and history — not just content.
Recovery tip
Your backup strategy has to account for people — the tired ones, the rushed ones, the overcon-
fident ones, and the well-meaning ones who just made a bad change. Use external, versioned 
backups that can restore clean data, metadata, and relationships even when the disruption came 
from legitimate access.

Mishaps at your cloud provider
Cloud providers are highly resilient, but not infallible. Service failures, configuration defects, synchronization 
issues, control-plane bugs, and cascading dependencies can affect availability, access, or trust in produc-
tion data even when the customer did nothing wrong. These events belong in their own category because 
the root cause sits with the provider, but the operational consequences still land squarely on the customer.
Microsoft’s Azure Front Door post-incident review from late October 2025 is a useful example. According 
to Microsoft, a specific sequence of valid customer configuration changes across different control-plane 
build versions generated incompatible metadata that exposed a latent bug in the Azure Front Door data 
plane. The resulting failure caused connection timeouts and DNS resolution issues across Azure Front Door 
and Azure CDN, with knock-on impact to Microsoft 365, Dynamics 365, Power Platform, Entra ID services, 
and even support-case creation. This detail matters because it shows how mature cloud environments can 
still fail in non-obvious ways: not through one dramatic hardware outage, but through interaction between 
control-plane versions, metadata, and protection logic.
The incident is also a reminder that resiliency safeguards have limits. Microsoft noted that configuration 
protections and “last known good” controls did not stop the problem because the metadata passed available 
checks before the asynchronous failure surfaced. Recovery required manual intervention, blocking further 
configuration propagation, editing the last-known-good configuration, and phased recovery across the 
fleet. For customers, the takeaway is not that hyperscalers are unreliable, but rather that customer continuity 
cannot depend entirely on the assumption that provider-side safeguards will always behave as designed.
This is where shared responsibility stops being a slogan and becomes an operating model. The provider is 
responsible for the infrastructure and service availability it delivers, while the customer is still responsible 
for data, identities, configurations, retention decisions, backup practices, and recovery readiness. Not 
every provider incident is a direct data-loss event: some are availability or access-path incidents. But from 
the customer’s perspective, the question is the same: Can critical data still be reached and recovered if the 
production platform is unstable or unavailable?
How control is regained
Treat provider-side incidents as continuity events, not somebody else’s problem. If the control plane 
is unstable, you still need an independent path to clean data and a plan for what business can oper-
ate from backup.
Recovery tip
Treat provider-side incidents as proof that availability and recoverability are different problems. 
Your vendor restores the service, but you still need an independent recovery path for the business 
data, identities, and configurations that keep operations moving.

Migration gone bad
Migration-related loss is usually not dramatic until it’s expensive. It happens when users, mailboxes, files, 
permissions, collaboration spaces, or identity objects move from one environment to another and the 
move introduces omissions, duplication, corruption, or data mixing that is difficult to unwind after the fact. 
In Microsoft 365 especially, tenant-to-tenant moves tied to mergers, acquisitions, divestitures, and reorga-
nization are common enough that Microsoft treats them as standard migration scenarios, not edge cases.
A realistic example is pharmaceutical divestiture. A business unit is sold, and a defined set of employees, 
mailboxes, OneDrive data, SharePoint sites, groups, and permissions must move into a new tenant. Before 
the migration even starts, both sides have reasons to verify exactly what belongs where. If the migration fails 
halfway through, if content is omitted, or if data from the retained business is mixed into the divested envi-
ronment, the organization needs a known-good record of the source state. Without that reference point, 
it becomes much harder to prove what should have moved, what should have stayed behind, and whether 
sensitive or commercially important information ended up in the wrong place.

That’s why backup matters in a migration even when no attack has taken place. It creates a trusted pre-mi-
gration baseline that can be used to validate the move, investigate discrepancies, and restore clean source 
data if the project introduces errors. In carve-outs and divestitures, that baseline is not just technically 
useful, it can become legally important. Missing, misplaced, or over-shared data can quickly turn into 
disputes about what was actually transferred.
How control is regained
•	Take a validated point-in-time backup before cutover
•	Keep a trustworthy source baseline until the migration is complete and verified.
Recovery tip
Treat migrations like disaster scenarios. Take complete, point-in-time backups before cutover, 
decide what must be provable afterward, and test recovery as part of the migration plan. Other-
wise, you may discover that yesterday’s lunch menu is easy to restore while the data the business 
actually needs is still tied up in the project.

Resilience is backup + tested recovery
Backup isn’t a checkbox. Disaster recovery resilience is immutable, independent backup plus tested, veri-
fied recovery. A backup that exists in theory but has never been exercised under realistic conditions is still 
only part of the job. Backup and recovery become resilience when the organization has tested the incidents 
it’s actually likely to face, measured the results, and improved the process. Backup is necessary, but it 
becomes operationally meaningful only when the business has proved it can recover with it.
Practiced recovery is rarely a single technical action. More often, it’s a sequence of dependencies, deci-
sions, and timing constraints. An organization needs to know what must come back first, which business 
processes depend on that order, how identity and content interact, and whether acceptable recovery 
objectives can actually be met in practice rather than assumed in theory. Testing is what exposes weak 
sequencing, fuzzy ownership, and hidden dependencies before an incident does.
The right tests are scenario-based. That means exercising recovery from the incidents the organization is 
actually likely to face: ransomware, large-scale admin error, provider-side disruption, identity corruption, 
and bulk restore — not just single-item recovery. A test that proves someone can restore one deleted file 
may still say almost nothing about whether the business can recover a tenant-wide failure or a compromised 
identity layer. Importantly, bulk restore isn’t theoretical: Keepit’s Annual Data Report shows 9 in 10 enterprise 
and commercial-size organizations validate bulk recovery at least once a year —suggesting that at scale, 
bulk restore testing is already a standard marker of recovery maturity.”

Identity deserves its own testing track. In Microsoft 365 environments, Entra ID is the control layer for access, 
roles, policies, groups, app registrations, and service principals. Testing should therefore not stop at files,

relationships can be rebuilt in a separate clean tenant if the primary tenant is compromised, unavailable, or 
simply no longer trustworthy. That’s why cross-tenant restore is more of a control question than a product 
feature discussion.

 Resilience is backup + tested recovery

Keepit’s Annual Data Report flags identity recovery as a persistent blind spot: Organizations test identity 
restores roughly four times less often than productivity data restores. That gap matters because if Entra ID 
can’t be recovered cleanly, access to Microsoft 365, and often every other SaaS system federated to it, may 
be blocked even when the underlying data is intact. 
It’s also worth noting that this comparison is drawn from organizations that already have an Entra ID back-
up/recovery capability in place—so it reflects a gap that can persist even among teams that are relatively 
advanced in their recovery posture.
Testing is also getting harder to separate from governance. Whether the driver is DORA, SEC expectations, 
FINRA expectations, or internal audit pressure, the direction is the same: Organizations increasingly need 
to show not only that data is protected, but that it can be restored in a controlled, reliable way when need-
ed. Navigating SEC and FINRA regulations makes the same point from a different angle: Recovery testing 
provides operational proof. It shows that backup is not just a policy artifact or a retention mechanism, but a 
workable part of the resilience strategy — and that the organization can recover on terms it understands and 
controls.
Recovery tip
Treat testing as a standing resilience practice rather than a one-time validation exercise. 
The goal is to confirm that backup data is independent, intact, and usable in realistic scenarios — and 
that the organization can restore the right data and identity elements in the right sequence when an 
incident occurs.

From testing cadence to recovery maturity

SaaS disaster recovery maturity
That’s why maturity matters. For many organizations, the problem is not understanding that disaster recovery 
is important, it’s knowing where the current capability really stands, where the biggest gaps are, and what 
the next most valuable improvement should be. A SaaS-specific maturity framework is useful because resil-
ience isn’t so black and white as a yes or no: An organization can have backups and still lack clear ownership, 
scenario coverage, documentation, or sovereignty-aware recovery design.
Figure 6. A practical maturity path moves teams from reactive recovery toward managed resilience.  
Related framework: SaaS disaster recovery maturity framework.
Keepit’s SaaS disaster recovery maturity framework uses five stages — reactive, basic, structured, proac-
tive, and managed — to make that progression tangible. Reactive teams recover ad hoc, depend on individ-
ual heroics, and often discover the process while the incident is happening. Basic teams have some back-
ups and some repeatable steps, but coverage and testing are inconsistent. Structured teams document 
procedures, assign owners, and bring regular testing into the process. Proactive teams measure, improve, 
and exercise scenarios before crisis reveals the gaps. Managed teams integrate recovery with governance, 
compliance, communications, and broader business continuity.

From testing cadence to recovery maturity

One of the strengths of a maturity model is that it discourages perfection theater. Most organizations 
do not become ‘managed’ by buying one more tool. They advance by moving to the next stage on 
purpose: documenting recovery procedures, clarifying responsibilities, identifying business-critical 
SaaS data and identity dependencies, introducing more regular and realistic testing, and then building 
measurement and continuous improvement on top of that foundation.
Maturity is also rarely uniform. An organization can be strong on executive motivation or compliance pressure 
and still be weak on documentation, staffing, or scenario coverage. The point of the model is not to award a 
flattering label. It is to help the organization orient itself honestly and choose the next improvement that will 
make the biggest operational difference.

Governance and sovereignty 
define the terms of control
Data sovereignty has moved from a niche legal concern to a broader resilience and governance issue. It’s 
no longer just a question of where data sits on a map, but rather a question of which laws apply to it, who 
can compel access to it, which providers and sub-processors sit in the dependency chain, and whether the 
organization retains meaningful control over how data is protected and recovered.
The shared responsibility and sovereignty discussion captures the shift well: Shared responsibility answers 
who owns the risk; sovereignty shapes the conditions under which that risk has to be managed. That distinc-
tion matters because sovereignty is often misunderstood as simple residency. In practice, residency is 
only one layer of the issue. Data may be created in one country, processed in another, stored in a third, and 
backed up in a fourth. And for data governance, organizations need to know what data exists, which retention 
and compliance obligations apply, who can access it, which provider and sub-processor dependencies sit 
behind it, and whether recovery can happen without recreating the same exposure. If the production SaaS 
provider and the backup provider depend on the same underlying hyperscaler or legal jurisdiction, an orga-
nization may have redundancy without true independence. That’s why the broader data sovereignty report 
treats sovereignty as part of resilience planning, not as a separate compliance checkbox.

A useful way to think about this is that sovereignty introduces a second layer of recovery readiness. The first 
layer asks whether data can be restored, while the second asks whether it is stored (and can be restored) 
under conditions the organization controls and can trust: in the right jurisdiction, with clear access, and with-
out being trapped by the same dependencies that may have contributed to the original disruption. If the 
answer to the first question is yes but the answer to the second is unclear, resilience is still incomplete.
Put differently, shared responsibility says you still own the data risk, and sovereignty says you also own the 
rules and obligations attached to that data. Together, they determine whether you control your SaaS data or 
not in a much broader sense.
Questions worth asking now
	
□
Who controls the infrastructure on which both production and backup depend?
	
□
Which jurisdictions can assert authority over the data or the backup provider?
	
□
Are production and backup tied to the same hyperscaler, region, or legal dependency chain?
	
□
Can the organization prove those answers when an incident, audit, or geopolitical event makes 
them matter?

Conclusion
The central lesson of SaaS disaster recovery is simple: The cloud changed the location of the 
problem, not the consequences of losing data, as well as introducing the shared responsibility 
models which all SaaS providers employ. Whether the trigger is malicious, mistaken, provid-
er-side, or migration-related, the business still has to answer the same core questions under 
pressure: Can we access clean data, can we restore it in the right order, and can we do so in an 
environment we trust and control?
The four Ms of data loss show how control is lost, while resilience shows how control is regained 
— through immutable, independent backup, practiced recovery, disciplined governance, and 
sovereignty-aware design. The organizations that get there are not necessarily the ones with 
the boldest claims about resilience, they’re the ones that understand data loss happens, test 
realistic scenarios, include identity and bulk restore in those tests, know where they sit on the 
maturity curve, and treat sovereignty and dependency questions as part of the design rather 
than as afterthoughts.
Independent, sovereign backup plus tested, verified recovery is disaster recovery resilience. 
And in SaaS, resilience is ultimately about control: knowing your data is protected, recoverable, 
and governed on terms you know and can trust.
