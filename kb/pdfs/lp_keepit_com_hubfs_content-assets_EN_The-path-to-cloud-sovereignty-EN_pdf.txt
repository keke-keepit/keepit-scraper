From principles to practical evaluation
Most regulatory frameworks define what risks you need to manage, but not how to assess them consis-
tently. The Cloud Sovereignty Framework bridges that gap by helping you to answer, in practice, a simple 
question: “Can we rely on this provider from a sovereignty and control perspective?” The framework helps 
you answer that question in a structured, defensible way by guiding you through three key steps, which 
together turn what could be a subjective assessment into a consistent, repeatable evaluation process:
INTRODUCTION
Keepit’s interpretation of EU cloud sovereignty
In line with the European Commission’s Cloud Sovereignty Framework, Keepit understands cloud sovereignty 
not as technical isolation, but as the practical ability for EU customers to retain control over their data, 
operations, and legal position when using cloud services. This includes: 
•	
Control over jurisdiction, governance, and access to data 
•	
Protection against unintended extraterritorial access 
•	
Operational resilience and independence 
•	
Transparency of technology and supply chain dependencies
In this guide, we will present the eight sovereignty objectives (SOV-1 to SOV-8) and summarize how Keepit 
addresses each of these objectives. By doing so, we want to describe how we ensure that customer data 
remains under effective European control, protected from undue external influence, and managed in 
accordance with EU law and values.
Kim Larsen 
Group Chief Information Security Officer, Keepit
1. Breaking down sovereignty into concrete areas of evaluation
Instead of treating sovereignty as a single concept, you assess it across distinct dimensions such 
as legal exposure, operational control, and data handling, called “sovereignty objectives” in the 
framework.
2. Examining the contributing factors within each area
Each dimension is supported by specific factors that help you evaluate real-world risks — for 
example, exposure to foreign jurisdictions, level of operational independence, or dependency on 
external supply chains. These are called “contributing factors” in the EU framework.
3. Assessing both minimum acceptability and overall strength
You determine whether a provider meets the minimum required level in each area (“sovereignty 
effectiveness”) and then evaluate how strong they are overall to support comparison between 
vendors (“sovereignty score”).

OBJECTIVE 1:
Ownership, control, and alignment with the EU ecosystem 
Strategic Sovereignty asks a more fundamental question than where your data is stored: Who actually 
controls the company running your cloud service, and could that change under pressure? It looks at 
where decision-making authority sits, how exposed the provider is to a change of ownership or control, 
how much of its funding and economic footprint is EU-based, and whether it could keep operating under 
external pressure.

OBJECTIVE 2:
Exposure to legal systems and data access risks

hand over your data and under what circumstances. This objective evaluates which country’s law actually 
governs your provider’s operations and contracts, how exposed that provider is to foreign laws with 
extraterritorial reach (such as the U.S. CLOUD Act), and whether legal, contractual, or technical channels 
exist that could let a non-EU authority access your data or systems.

From framework to decision
As European organizations place increasing emphasis on sovereignty, many are reassessing their reliance 
on global cloud ecosystems. Moving toward a more EU-anchored technology stack can help reduce legal 
and operational exposure — but it also introduces trade-offs. Shifting to regional providers may require 
upfront investment and careful consideration of resilience, innovation, and long-term flexibility.
This is where the EU Cloud Sovereignty Framework becomes valuable. It provides a structured way to 
evaluate providers across legal, operational, and technical dimensions — helping organizations not only 
meet regulatory expectations, but also demonstrate control over data, infrastructure, and third-party risk. 
The challenge, however, remains identifying providers that strengthen sovereignty without compromising 
performance or scalability.
