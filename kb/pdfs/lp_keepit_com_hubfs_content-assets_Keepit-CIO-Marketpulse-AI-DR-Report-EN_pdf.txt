Organizations are rapidly 
adopting agentic AI. In a recent 
survey conducted by Foundry, 
more than half report active AI 
implementations, while the rest are 
piloting or evaluating initiatives.
Given the pace of AI adoption, it’s 
critical to ensure your business is 
resilient and can recover quickly 
when an incident occurs. AI agents 
typically operate autonomously 
across SaaS environments and 
interact with multiple systems at 
once. If granted broad permissions, 
an AI agent may update data across 
several systems simultaneously, 
allowing errors to propagate before 
teams have a chance to intervene.
These characteristics heighten 
the risks of inadvertent data 
exposure or loss. Evidence of AI-
related risk is already emerging, 
according to IBM research1, which 
found that 13% of organizations 
have already reported breaches 
involving AI models or applications.
Despite these risks, most IT leaders 
(94%) say they’re confident their 
current disaster recovery (DR) plan 
covers agentic AI systems. And 
yet, only 32% test their DR plans 
on a monthly basis. Considering 
the speed at which AI is heading 
into production, there is a gap 
between perceived readiness and 
real-world recovery capability.
AI adoption moves 
beyond IT
Most IT and security leaders 
report being well-informed about 
AI deployments. But as agentic AI 
adoption spreads from centralized 
IT teams into the wider organization, 
even small blind spots can disrupt 
incident response and recovery.
About the research
Foundry surveyed 301 IT decision-
makers in Australia, France, Germany, 
New Zealand, the UK, and the US, at 
companies with 1,000+ employees. 
Respondents have roles in IT 
networking, and/or security, and 
make decisions around AI services 
and platforms. The online survey was 
sponsored by Keepit and conducted 
from Nov. 19 to Dec. 15, 2025.

The respondents recognize that 
agentic environments can introduce 
new challenges around disaster 
recovery. They are concerned, for 
example, about not understanding 
AI’s risks, no accountability for AI 
systems, and low organizational 
awareness (see Figure 1).  
“AI was originally brought into the 
organization through IT,” says Kim 
Larsen, CISO, Keepit. “Now, individual 
teams are deploying it themselves as 
they look to automate their processes 
and workloads. That can move things 
forward quickly, but it also makes it 
more challenging to see how systems 
and data connect—and know what 
to do when something goes wrong.”
Governance matters 
for recovery
In agentic AI environments, 
governance guides how 
responsibility, decision-making, and 
recovery actions are coordinated 
when issues arise. However, the 
results show that controls and 
governance are not keeping pace 
with the rapid adoption of AI.

For example, 33% of IT and 
security leaders say they 
only have partial control 
over the use of agentic 
AI in their organizations. 
“In real-world recovery 
scenarios, the problem 
is rarely the absence of 
backups,” Larsen says. “It’s 
knowing who is in charge 
of the recovery and which 
systems are restored first 
when multiple systems are affected. 
When decisions are delayed, recovery 
takes longer than necessary.”
Backup does not 
equal recovery
Data protection is widely 
recognized as essential; 91% 
report having dedicated SaaS 
backup or DR solutions in place. 
However, this widespread adoption 
does not translate into uniformly 
high confidence in recovery abilities. 
More than half (51%) say they are 
either not confident or only somewhat 
confident that their existing DR 
plans cover scenarios involving 
agentic AI or automation tools. 
“What often surprises teams is how 
many steps sit between having a 
backup and actually being operational 
again when an incident occurs,” Larsen 
says. “Restoring data is just one part of 
the process. Reconnecting systems, 
validating data, and confirming that 
workflows function correctly can 
take far longer than teams expect.”
Furthermore, only 56% place a 
high priority on protecting SaaS 
data and DR when implementing 
AI solutions. This pattern extends 
to testing practices; only 32% test 
their DR plans on a monthly basis. 
Considering the autonomous 
nature of agentic AI, quarterly or 
annual testing is insufficient.

“Low testing frequency may be 
enough for more static environments,” 
Larsen says. “But as agentic AI 
introduces faster change and more 
interconnected systems, teams 
often need to test more frequently 
to ensure they’re prepared. Testing 
isn’t just about meeting a schedule. 
It’s how teams discover how 
failures spread and how well they 
can respond when it matters.”
The constraints holding 
back true DR
Similar to infrequent DR testing, only 
41% of respondents say that they have 
significantly changed their approach 
to disaster recovery as a result of AI 
adoption. Despite acknowledging that 
agentic AI is a game-changer for the 
business, there is little evidence that IT 
and security teams are keeping pace 
with how AI is affecting data protection 
and mitigating potential risks.

However, they are facing substantial 
challenges. Asked to rank the top 
three barriers to achieving a resilient 
SaaS disaster recovery strategy, IT 
leaders cited a limited understanding 
of AI’s impact on DR planning, 
budget or resource constraints, 
and a lack of internal skills.
As shown in Figure 2, there isn’t 
really one dominant challenge. 
Enterprises are facing a host of 
challenges, which may explain the 
lag between AI deployment and the 
evolution of recovery planning.
“Teams often struggle because 
there isn’t a shared understanding 
of what ‘good’ recovery looks like in 
an AI-driven environment,” Larsen 
says. “Without that reference 
point, it’s hard to decide where to 
focus—whether on tools, training, 
processes, or ownership—and 
efforts tend to become fragmented, 
which dilutes progress.”
Build resilience through 
stronger disaster 
recovery planning
Taken together, the Foundry research 
findings point to a consistent theme: 
While backup is common, recovery 
capability is less consistently 
understood, tested, or validated. This 
gap affects an organization’s ability 
to attain operational resilience. 
“Resilience starts with clarity,” Larsen 
says. “You need to know what matters 
most, who leads recovery, and where 
the weak points really are. Teams 
that plan and practice regularly 
don’t have to figure things out in the 
moment; they follow the plan.” 
The organizations that succeed 
with AI aren’t just the ones that 
are able to deploy quickly; they’re 
the ones that build resilience into 
every stage of adoption, ensuring 
the business remains operational 
even when systems fail.
