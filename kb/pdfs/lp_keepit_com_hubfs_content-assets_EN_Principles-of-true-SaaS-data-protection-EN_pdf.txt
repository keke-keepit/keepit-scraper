Introduction
SaaS has become the operational backbone of most businesses, but most organizations 
are not protecting the underlying data the way they protect data across the rest of the IT 
landscape. Customer records, financial transactions, and operational decisions all live 
in SaaS applications today, and business is disrupted or stops the moment that data are 
wrong, missing, or unreachable.
AI further amplifies these points of failure. As agents and automations act directly on SaaS 
data — reading it, summarizing it, deciding from it — the same data that once just supported 
daily work now also drives decisions and actions no person may ever check. Where previous 
data protection focused simply on securing backup as insurance for a worst-case scenario; 
now the data in that backup must also serve as a source of truth that organizations — and 
their AI — can trust.
Today, a substantial gap exists between what SaaS vendors protect and what businesses 
assume is protected, and it leaves most organizations exposed — not because SaaS data 
aren’t seen as a key component of business continuity, but because the cost and steps of 
protecting it haven’t been made clear.

The history of backup
The practice of creating duplicate copies of important records for 
verification and protection dates back to ancient civilizations of 
Mesopotamia and Egypt, where scribes produced parallel copies of 
administrative and legal documents to prevent loss and tampering. 
In the Roman Empire, additional redundancy was introduced with 
centrally stored copies supplementing local city archives, as these 
were commonly destroyed during uprisings. The process and 
principles were further refined through centuries: monasteries 
copying manuscripts, the printing press enabling mass replication, 
microfilm compacting archives for secure off-site storage, and 
physical vaults protecting the most sensitive records.
Duplication, off-site distribution, and physical hardening changed 
shape with every era, documenting that the approach to backup 
has gone through massive technological shifts: from handwriting 
to the printing press to digital, and from paper to tape to cloud 
storage. Technology changes; the purpose of backup and the logic 
of resilience do not.
In the computing era, backup has evolved in parallel with IT 
architecture. Backups started as complete 1:1 system dumps, but as 
file systems and databases emerged, the data layer was separated 
from the system. Backing up data only gave more flexibility and 
required less storage, but made full system restores cumbersome 
and slow. Virtualization changed the unit of protection to virtual 
machines, which maintained file-level flexibility and made full 
system restores much easier. 
The introduction of cloud computing, and SaaS in particular, 
introduced another profound change — the decoupling of data 
from the organization’s own infrastructure. This shift introduces a 
fundamentally different challenge: it is not just a new technology, 
but a new model of control, responsibility, and risk.
Backup and recovery has always been an essential part of how organizations and 
institutions safeguard critical assets. Technology may change, but the need for 
resilience remains constant.

From passive insurance to active enabler
Although the primary role has been disaster recovery, backup data are now well-positioned to be the 
trusted foundation organizations can always recover from, whether it’s a single file today or the whole 
system tomorrow.
We already see that recovery needs are not limited to disaster recovery. Keepit Annual Data Report 2026(1)
shows that single file downloads account for 90% of all restore operations, with the remaining 10% almost 
evenly split between in-place restores and bulk downloads.
Keeepit Annual Data Report 2026 (1)
This tells us that most recovery needs are granular and immediate: a 
deleted file, a modified document, or a corrupted permission. The 
business need is not to rebuild systems, but to retrieve a specific 
item to ensure operational resiliency. Which suggests that recovery 
has shifted from catastrophic to operational.
As society, businesses, and technology continuously change, data 
protection and resilience will play an even more critical role than it 
does today. Gartner®  predicts that “by 2029, 30% of enterprises 
will integrate backup copies as a data source for analytics and 
inference, up from less than 5% in 2025.” 
(2)
This would suggest that the purpose of backup doesn’t stop at 
operational resilience, but includes protecting the data acting as the 
“single source of truth”. Preserving the trusted data foundation from 
which AI can safely reason in turn ensures efficiency improvements 
and business innovation.

The surveys from CIO and McKinsey also emphasize a critical finding: adoption of AI has outpaced the risk 
controls needed to manage it safely. Organizations are moving faster on AI adoption than on the governance, 
testing, and accountability structures needed to manage its risks.
This mismatch does not only add new complexities and threats, but also makes existing exposures more 
apparent and imminent in an AI context.
While it might take hours or days for a person to enter faulty data across a few systems, an AI agent can 
execute thousands of write operations across multiple systems within minutes, spreading an error before 
anyone notices. This shrinks the window for detection and correction, turning issues that would normally 
surface gradually into large-scale problems almost instantly.
Therefore, data protection again emerges as a critical priority, but the obstacles organizations face are 
markedly different. This is because the data that fuel both AI initiatives and run the entire organization do not 
live within an organization’s infrastructure but instead sit inside SaaS applications outside the organization’s 
direct control. Most data protection strategies have not adapted to this reality.
The SaaS data protection gap
Despite the business value of SaaS data, backup and recovery capabilities are often inadequate and a blind 
spot in companies’ resiliency setups. While organizations increasingly recognize SaaS security as a priority 
— according to Cloud Security Alliance, 86% rank it as high or highest priority
(10) — many remain largely 
unprepared for data loss. 
Keepit’s own survey adds nuances to this. It confirms that SaaS data protection is a key priority, but it calls 
out two caveats: While currently a priority, less than a third (29%) of companies actually have a solution in 
place, and almost one fourth (24%) assume data protection is handled by the SaaS provider. This suggests 
that the principles of SaaS data protection are not fully understood. 
Keepit industry survey, February 2026, N=600 (11)

This is further emphasized by a CIO Market Pulse survey (12)  that finds that 37% of organizations rely on native 
data protection only, which reveals a genuine gap. Organizations understand that SaaS data matters, but 
they have not invested in purpose-built data protection solutions and assume native capabilities — designed 
to support platform uptime and short-term data retention only — will suffice for business continuity and 
disaster recovery.
Native SaaS retention isn’t backup. Short-term retention windows and previous-version history exist 
to support the SaaS application vendor’s own operations, not the range of loss, deletion, or corruption 
scenarios an organization actually needs to recover from. Treating these features as sufficient for business 
continuity is one of the most common and costly misreadings of their intended purpose.
Traditional backup tools, retrofitted onto SaaS, run into a different problem: they were built for infrastructure 
that organizations controlled directly — servers, virtual machines, on-premises applications — not for a 
model where the vendor owns the infrastructure and the application, and the customer only owns the data 
inside. That mismatch tends to surface as feature gaps and architectural workarounds: capabilities added 
on top of a foundation built for a different model entirely.
The gap is not caused by deliberate neglect but is a consequence of the unique challenges of protecting SaaS 
data: The data model, responsibility model, and risk model are all different from that of traditional backup.
Data without context are unusable
SaaS separates data from the application and removes user control of the underlying infrastructure. You 
cannot restore a working system. Instead, you must restore data following the application’s structure. This 
requires understanding where each element belongs and how it should be reassembled.
“SaaS data protection remains one of the biggest challenges 
in the data protection market for vendors and buyers alike.”

This requires backup to capture not just data but also metadata and context: ownership, permissions, 
sharing settings, folder structures, versions, labels, comments, and relationships. Without this context, 
restored data may be incomplete or unusable. Traditional backup captured files, systems, and databases. 
SaaS backup must preserve application-level meaning.
The shared responsibility model
SaaS providers secure infrastructure and application availability, while customers are meant to secure their 
own data and are responsible for data protection, governance, and recovery. This division is fundamental 
across all major SaaS providers.
Many organizations continue to misinterpret what shared responsibility means. When data are lost to 
accidental deletion, unwanted modification, or insider threats, SaaS vendors are not able to assist with 
restore. Those scenarios fall entirely within the customers’ responsibilities.  SaaS application customers 
create data, own data, and are responsible for protecting their data. Therefore, the ability to restore is tied 
directly to the decisions an organization makes on retention policies, backup practices, access controls, 
and recovery testing.
The risk scenarios are not system-dependent
Traditional data protection strategies address infrastructure failure, hardware loss, and ransomware attacks, 
and focus on full system restore. SaaS risk scenarios include accidental deletion, compromised accounts, 
misconfiguration, faulty integrations, automation errors, and loss of historical versions or permissions. SaaS 
vendors can also change application features, deprecate capabilities, or alter recovery options at will.
According to Cloud Security A lliance
(14), the most common reasons for SaaS-specific breaches are about 
governance and configuration, and not related to hardware failures, system-level access etc. The SaaS 
application itself can be fully operational, while the business cannot recover its data. Moreover, the risks are 
not application specific. As data flow between SaaS applications, compromised data in a single non-critical 
platform may have a ripple effect on a broad section of the business-critical SaaS ecosystem.
“However, the byproduct of these integrations is the development of 
complex interdependencies where a single service outage at a vendor can 
have major repercussions in the rest of the business.”
Forrester Research, “How To Create A SaaS Application Resilience Strategy, 2026” (15)
Cloud Security Alliance, State of  SaaS Security 2025-2026 (14)

Operational truth is exposed
SaaS applications don’t behave like the infrastructure backup was originally built to protect. The SaaS vendor 
controls the infrastructure and application, so simply running a backup cannot capture the full system that 
was traditionally possible, which means the risks that cause data loss are different from the failures backing 
up data has traditionally guarded against.
The result is that most organizations have no reliable way to know whether the SaaS data they depend on 
to feed decisions, automations, and AI can be trusted. Having a backup was supposed to be the guarantee 
that what you recovered was an exact copy. For SaaS data, that guarantee doesn’t currently hold — leaving 
businesses, and increasingly their AI, without a trusted foundation.
The future of SaaS data protection
The rush towards AI has led to a wakeup call for protecting SaaS data. Just a few years ago, most companies 
did not prioritize backing up SaaS data, and that share increased only incrementally. Now, we’re at an 
inflection point, as that share has accelerated sharply in the past year, and within just a few years, industry 
expectations point to the majority of companies treating it as a high priority.
“By 2028, 75% of enterprises will prioritize backup of SaaS 
applications as a critical requirement, compared to 15% in 2024.”
Gartner, Top Trends in Enterprise Backup and Recovery for 2024, April 1, 2024 (16)
“By 2029, 80% of enterprises will prioritize backup of SaaS 
applications as a critical requirement, compared with 20% in 2025.”
Gartner, Critical Capabilities for Backup and Data Protection, July 9, 2025 (17)
“By 2029, 75% of enterprises will treat SaaS application backup 
as a critical component of their data protection strategy, up from 
40% in 2026.”
Gartner, Market Guide for SaaS Backup, June 1, 2026 (18)
Awareness and prioritization of SaaS data protection are the critical first steps, but by themselves don’t 
close the gap. Exposure stems from applying protection models built for infrastructure that organizations 
controlled directly, to a model that works fundamentally differently. Therefore, losing the gap means 
adopting a set of principles built for the nuances of SaaS, and taking into consideration how it should be 
governed.
Leading industry experts agree on these principles. Across Tier 1 analyst firm research, four things stand out: 
know what SaaS you need to protect and how critical it is, know who owns that responsibility, understand 
the SaaS-specific requirements a solution must meet, and validate that the solution works.

Mapping data to business criticality
Customer records, financial transactions, legal documents, 
operational workflows, regulated data, and AI-relevant data carry 
different stakes if tampered with or lost. To attempt to protect all of 
it equally means protecting none of it well.
This calls for an honest inventory, not assumptions. Most 
organizations underestimate how much SaaS they actually require 
for business continuity. Organizations diligent in data governance 
routinely discover hundreds of applications that were never formally 
tracked, often brought in by individual teams to solve a specific 
problem and then left running long after anyone remembered they 
existed. You can’t protect what you don’t know you have.
An inventory only tells you what exists, not what matters. Mapping which SaaS applications and data support 
critical processes and assessing the business impact and dependencies is a critical classification exercise. 
At its core, it is about grouping data by risk profile so protection can be directed where it matters most. 
The Intelligent Data Governance
(19) report covers this in more depth, including a practical framework for 
classifying data by sensitivity and risk. 
Assigning ownership
SaaS data protection fails as often from unclear ownership as from a flawed strategy. Responsibility is easy 
to assume and easier to leave undocumented. Too often, the moment data are lost, is when IT, security, and 
the business unit running the application discover they assumed someone else had it covered.
While leadership is accountable for governance overall, day-to-day classification and prioritization 
decisions belong to the departments that own and use the data — in close collaboration with the people 
who consume it. The Intelligent Data Governance
(19)  report also discusses the need for ownership. 
Meeting the requirements of SaaS
Native retention and retrofitted infrastructure tools each fall short for different reasons. A solution built for 
SaaS specifically starts from how the platform actually works and what true protection looks like.
Built-for-purpose-tools are designed to capture all the necessary context to make a recovery usable rather 
than just a pile of data. Capturing the metadata, permissions, and relationships behind SaaS data means 
recoveries land in a working state, and retention information is kept for compliance and business policy 
adherence. The same context also makes backup data directly available and searchable, giving access to 
information without doing a full recovery.

Finally, SaaS-specific solutions typically support a broader range of applications and are simpler to use, as 
they have fewer configuration steps and generally require less specialized expertise. This means the tool 
stays manageable as the SaaS estate continues to grow.
Where data is stored matters as much as what gets captured. Separating backup data from the production 
environment reduces risk, as a single compromised account or misconfiguration cannot reach both the 
original data and its backup at once.
Real independence extends beyond the SaaS vendor to the hyperscaler hosting the platform and backup 
storage, as backup and production environments sharing the same cloud infrastructure undermines both 
independence and data sovereignty. This separation is not only about reducing the risk of data loss, but an 
unavoidable compliance requirement in regulated industries.

Validating the recovery process
Restore testing, recovery reporting, and audit evidence turn hope into verification and verification into 
confidence. Tabletop exercises that simulate real scenarios — a platform outage, a large-scale data loss, 
a compromised account — build that confidence further, surfacing what documentation alone can’t: how 
recovery workflows actually perform under pressure, and where dependencies between systems only 
become visible once something is being restored.
The payoff is preparedness that holds up when it’s tested for real, not just on paper.

The principles that close the 
protection gap
Closing this gap is not just a resilience exercise. The old principles of backup — redundancy, durability, 
independent copies — still hold. What no longer holds is the assumption they were built on: that protecting 
the whole system was enough. SaaS breaks that assumption, and closing the gap means adding to those 
principles by preserving context, and keeping backup genuinely independent of both the SaaS vendor and 
the infrastructure on which it runs.
This is the latest step in an evolution backup has always followed: from insurance against disaster, to a tool 
for operational recovery, to a verified source of truth for both human and AI decision-making. 
The immediate benefit of closing the SaaS data protection gap is straightforward: less exposure to data 
loss, faster recovery, fewer blind spots in a business that increasingly runs on SaaS. The longer-term value is 
greater. Organizations that close these gaps are building the one thing the AI era actually runs on: data they 
can trust.
