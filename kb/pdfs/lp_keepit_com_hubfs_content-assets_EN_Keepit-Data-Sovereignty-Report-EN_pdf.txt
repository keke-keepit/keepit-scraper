Introduction
Data sits at the center of both the biggest risks and the biggest opportunities facing your organization. 
CISOs and CIOs are responsible for safeguarding data with robust protections while also ensuring it’s 
available — in the right forms, to the right people, at the right time — to power both day-to-day operations 
and innovation initiatives.
For a number of reasons — including an unpredictable geopolitical landscape, the market dominance 
of a handful of hyperscalers, stricter privacy regulations, and uptime/availability requirements — digital 
sovereignty has rapidly emerged as a strategic priority. And data sovereignty, a necessary element 
of digital sovereignty, is driving organizations to take a closer look at cloud architectures, vendor 
dependence, the supply chain and use of sub-processors, and compliance requirements.
At a glance, digital sovereignty may seem to fall squarely under the purview of CISOs and legal counsel; 
however, its significance to your organization is such that digital sovereignty is more accurately regarded 
as a business issue of the utmost importance.
The world is changing, and — like it or not — these 
changes are forcing CEOs and boards to make 
decisions that have profound consequences.
For Keepit, digital sovereignty isn’t a theoretical 
ideal — it’s a cornerstone of our philosophy and 
our technology.

For IT leaders, this means autonomy: knowing 
exactly where your data is, who is handling it, and

In an era where political alliances change

A key element of digital sovereignty
Sovereignty means autonomy; that is, freedom from external control.
Digital sovereignty encompasses (in fact, requires) control over digital infrastructure, including data, 
operations, and technology (Figure 1). It’s about an entity’s ability to manage and control its digital 
environment — the freedom to store and run workloads wherever desired, for whatever reason (e.g., 
performance, resilience, compliance, etc.) — ensuring autonomy in the cyber realm. Digital sovereignty 
mitigates the risks associated with foreign data control or influence and consequently is a strategic 
imperative for aligning with regulatory frameworks within geographic boundaries.
Data sovereignty refers to the legal and regulatory control that a country or region has over data generated 
or residing within its borders. It dictates how data can be used, who can access it, and which laws apply to 
its handling. Data sovereignty ensures that data is subject to the laws of the country of origin.
Key takeaways:
• Data sovereignty refers to the legal and regulatory control over data, and is of 
vital importance for most organizations working on digital sovereignty.
• Digital sovereignty is a broad concept encompassing control over digital 
infrastructure — including data, hardware, and software.
• Data residency, which refers to the physical (geographic) location 
of data, is a key aspect of digital sovereignty.

Common data forms
Some of the most common and sensitive forms of data are:
•	 Intellectual property including trade secrets, algorithms, business 
process details, source code, and customer lists
•	 Internal and external communications, including text or chat messages, emails, 
voice calls, and videoconference recordings and transcripts
•	 Employee, customer/client, and third-party service provider data including personally identifiable 
information (PII), personal health information (PHI), and payment card information (PCI)
•	 Operational telemetry and sensor data, such as security events and 
capacity, performance, or load distribution data
•	 Identity and access management (IAM) data, including keys, tokens, and credentials
For most organizations pursuing digital sovereignty, achieving data sovereignty is the largest challenge. 
There are a number of reasons why this is the case, including:
•	 Today’s organizations work with massive volumes of data.
•	 Data exists in a wide range of forms, many of which are subject to regulatory requirements pertaining to 
privacy and security.
•	 Modern IT environments include complex combinations of public and private clouds, software-as-a-service 
(SaaS) applications, and on-premises infrastructure — plus local storage on devices such as laptops and 
mobile phones.
•	 Data is very easy to move, and tends to move around a lot during its lifecycle (Figure 2).
•	 Where data is stored or processed, what paths it traverses when it’s moved or used, and which companies play 
a role in these activities are often opaque.

Data residency refers to the physical location(s) 
where data is stored, processed, or otherwise 
handled.
Data residency is a key aspect of data sovereignty, 
for the straightforward reason that data residency 
often determines data sovereignty. That is, where 
data is held or used strongly influences — or even 
entirely dictates — associated legal obligations.
Moreover, today’s IT environments and data flows 
can be so complex that accurately understanding 
data residency can itself present a significant 
challenge. For example, data may be collected in 
one country, processed by a third party in another 
country, stored in another country, and backed up in 
yet another country. The data in such a scenario can 
be considered to reside in four countries, with four 
associated sets of data sovereignty obligations.
It’s also important to note that data residency is 
distinct from (but related to) data localization, with 
the latter referring to a legal requirement that data 
stay where it was created (or acquired). Where data 
localization laws apply, the causal chain extends 
one step further back: Data localization determines 
data residency, which then determines data 
sovereignty.
However, data residency requirements need not 
come from data localization requirements — many 
data residency requirements come from an 
organization’s internal policies or contractual 
obligations (Figure 3).

Geopolitics and hyperscaler dominance
Geopolitics and hyperscaler dominance bring data sovereignty to the forefront. 
As of November 2024, 97% of the cloud infrastructure and platform services market was dominated by US 
and Chinese hyperscalers:
Unfortunately, the global cloud market is not just about technology — increasingly, it’s about politics and 
control.
Dependency upon these few players leaves organizations worldwide vulnerable to political shifts and 
foreign legislation, and today’s organizations find themselves caught between:
•	
National security and law enforcement acts (e.g., the United States’ Clarifying Lawful Overseas Use of Data Act or 
the CLOUD Act) that compel access to data, even if it resides in foreign datacenters
•	
Privacy regulations (e.g., the European Union’s General Data Protection Act or GDPR) that require organizations 
to prevent data from being accessed by, among others, foreign entities

II ruling issued in July 20201 invalidated the EU-US Privacy Shield framework, in part because of a 
determination that US laws2  had several shortcomings that impeded the protection of personal data and 
violated the GDPR.

of EU citizens being accessed by the US government and its various agencies. Now, however, EU-based

Digital sovereignty as a legal protection mechanism
In the face of such a dilemma (e.g., complying with US laws and violating local ones, or the opposite), digital 
sovereignty represents an organization’s ability to ensure autonomy and security in a constantly shifting 
political and cyber landscape.
In response to concerns from EU-based 
organizations, US-based hyperscalers 
have launched offerings (e.g., Microsoft 
365 EU Data Boundary, Amazon’s European 
Sovereign Cloud, Google’s Sovereign 
Controls) and run marketing campaigns 
oriented around data remaining in the EU.
However, the CLOUD Act makes it very clear 
that the location of data doesn’t matter, 
bypassing GDPR protections. 
For example, Article 48 of the GDPR states:
“Any judgment of a court or tribunal and 
any decision of an administrative authority 
of a third country requiring a controller or 
processor to transfer or disclose personal 
data may only be recognized or enforceable 
in any manner if based on an international 
agreement, such as a mutual legal assistance 
treaty, in force between the requesting third 
country and the Union or a Member State, 
without prejudice to other grounds for 
transfer pursuant to this Chapter.”
In contrast, the CLOUD Act:
•
Allows extraterritorial reach of US law
•
Does not require judicial review under EU 
legal systems
•
Does not provide adequate legal remedies 
for EU citizens
This clear contradiction is causing EU-based 
organizations with operations in the US to 
focus less on where data is stored and more 
on who controls the infrastructure in which 
data is stored.
Ultimately, if the infrastructure provider 
falls under US jurisdiction, then there is no 
guarantee of full GDPR compliance.

Within individual nations or regulatory jurisdictions, sovereignty requirements are seen as a way to protect 
organizations — including the data they use and the workloads they run — from legal obligations imposed 
by external authorities.
In short, achieving digital sovereignty (and, by definition, data sovereignty) is expected to provide some 
degree of immunity from foreign laws or regulations that are at odds with local ones.
Secondary factors driving digital sovereignty initiatives
Looking beyond geopolitics, there are several 
other reasons why digital sovereignty is fast 
becoming a top-of-mind issue for organizations.
Supply chain risk
In recent years, organizations have taken a 
greater security-centric interest in their software 
supply chain due to a number of large-scale 
security incidents traced to compromised 
vendors and code repositories.
However, cloud infrastructure has largely 
avoided the same degree of scrutiny, despite an 
abundance of similar issues.
For instance, if a cloud identity provider (IdP) 
such as Microsoft or Okta suffers an outage 
due to an issue with the hyperscaler upon which 
they depend, then countless organizations will 
be rendered largely inoperable. (And while the 
impact may be smaller for most other SaaS 
outages, the general point still applies.)
“No problem,” leaders may think, “we have 
backups that will allow us to quickly recover and 
restore operations.”
Unfortunately, most SaaS backup solutions rely on the same hyperscalers as the SaaS providers. If a SaaS 
provider and backup provider use the same hyperscaler, then there’s a real problem: There’s no air gap. 
In certain scenarios (e.g., a natural disaster or other issue with a datacenter), geographic redundancy 
provides some measure of safety, but outages due to misconfigurations and errors have the potential to 
span multiple zones.
In contrast, sovereign backup providers provide a solution for fast, complete recovery by maintaining data 
availability, eliminating cross-border data replication through regional data centers, and minimizing cloud 
dependency via independent clouds.
Hybrid warfare
Today’s organizations operate in a very different global political and technological environment than their 
predecessors.

Hybrid warfare blends cyberattacks, physical sabotage, disinformation, and economic coercion to 
disrupt or destabilize its targets, and the ease with which digital infrastructure can be targeted — coupled 
with the outsized impact caused by disruptions — puts hyperscalers and important SaaS applications 
squarely in the sights of adversaries.
For example,  an advisory issued by the FBI in August 2025 warned that Salt Typhoon — an advanced 
persistent threat actor believed to be operated by China’s Ministry of State Security (MSS) — targeted 
more than 600 organizations across 80 countries.
Consequently, a crisis, conflict, or even merely a test run (with plausible deniability) may render cloud-
based data inaccessible.
Data sovereignty provides added resilience against this new reality, making it essential for an 
organization’s risk management and long-term digital strategy.
Availability and recovery requirements
Increasingly, organizations are finding themselves beholden to requirements relating to availability and 
recovery and are recognizing that dependence upon third-party providers represents a risk.
While some of these obligations stem from contractual service level agreements (SLAs), many originate 
with regulatory bodies. For example, under the EU’s Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA)  and 
Network and Information Security 2 (NIS2) directive, organizations in critical sectors must prove that they 
can recover from information and communication technology (ICT) disruptions with minimal reliance on 
third-party connectivity.

Countries and regions are investing heavily in their own cloud data 
centers, AI factories, and supercomputing capacity so they are less 
dependent on foreign providers. For instance, in Europe, there are 
efforts to build “AI gigafactories” and to push for sovereign cloud 
offerings.
Hybrid architectures (mix of on-premises, edge, and cloud) may 
become more common as a way to balance regulatory compliance, 
performance, latency, and control — yes, we are seeing a movement 
back to being on-premises in certain scenarios.
Regulations — like the recently adopted EU Data Act, the EU AI Act, 
GDPR, and various national laws — are increasing requirements around 
data privacy, transparency of training data, risk assessments of AI 
models, etc.
Laws governing cross-border data flows, laws that grant data 
subjects certain rights (e.g., to know how data is used, to port their 
data, etc.), and restrictions on foreign (non-local) access to critical 
data (or infrastructure) are becoming more common around the globe.
A lot of digital and data sovereignty depends on physical infrastructure: 
chips, GPUs/TPUs, networking, servers, etc. Many countries are at risk 
because production of key hardware is concentrated in a few places 
(e.g., Taiwan, US, and China).
Moves to develop domestic or regional fabrication, design, 
and manufacturing capabilities are increasing. Concerns about 
dependencies (e.g., for rare earths, semiconductor tooling, etc.) are 
becoming strategic and political issues.

Beyond “just data location,” there is growing concern about who 
controls what memories or learned behavior an AI can use, how 
personalized assistants track and store user history, etc. This touches 
on “cognitive sovereignty” — ensuring that users or nations retain 
control over their narratives, personal profiles, etc.
There are increasingly prominent demands for AI systems to be 
designed such that memory is portable, transparent, auditable, and 
controllable.
The global AI ecosystem is increasingly splitting along geopolitical 
lines. For example, trade restrictions (export controls), sanction 
regimes, or simply policy divergence are pushing infrastructure, 
standards, and supply chains to align differently in, say, the US/EU 
bloc vs. China vs. others.
Regions want legal and technical standards (for privacy, transparency, 
and safety) that reflect their values, which may diverge. This can lead 
to “splinternets” or digital divergence.
As AI becomes more embedded in public services, healthcare, justice, 
etc., there is stronger demand from citizens and civil society that AI 
be trustworthy, explainable, and non-biased, and for clarity over who 
owns intellectual property, who steers decisions, etc.
Embedding AI models and inference capabilities within edge devices 
(or locally, on-premises) rather than relying almost solely upon 
centralized clouds helps reduce dependency, latency, and exposure 
of sensitive data.
Self-sovereign identity systems and decentralized agents (sometimes 
using blockchain or trusted hardware) are becoming more common, 
giving more control over data and identity to individuals or smaller 
entities.
Building up large AI infrastructure (data centers, high performance 
compute) demands a lot of electricity, cooling, etc. Sovereign 
infrastructure must take into account energy availability, carbon 
footprint, and resilience — otherwise, environmental constraints 
can become the weak link and make digital sovereignty difficult to 
achieve.
