As a company dedicated to keeping business-critical SaaS data safe and always available, 
no matter the incident, Keepit occupies a unique vantage point in the global data pro-
tection landscape. Every day, our platform processes millions of real‑world backup and 
restore operations across customers, regions, and SaaS applications. This gives us direct 
insight into how organizations actually protect, access, and recover their SaaS data — not 
how they intend to, or how they believe they do.
The Keepit Annual Data Report is built on that operational reality. It’s based on aggregated 
and anonymized  behavioral data drawn from our production backup environment, reflect-
ing backup and recovery activity across all active Keepit data center regions: Denmark, 
Germany, Switzerland, UK, Australia, Canada, and USA. At no point does the analysis rely on 
surveys, self‑reported behavior, or individual customer cases. Instead, it examines observ-
able patterns: how often data is backed up, how frequently restores occur, what types of 
recovery actions are initiated, and how backup systems behave under everyday — as well 
as unusual — conditions.
We believe this perspective is relevant well beyond Keepit’s customer base. Backup and 
recovery are often discussed in abstract terms — policies, architectures, or compliance 
requirements — while the day‑to‑day mechanics of data recovery remain poorly under-
stood. By focusing on what actually happens when organizations restore data, this report 
aims to ground those discussions in evidence.
Our goal is not to prescribe best practices or rank maturity, but to contribute transparency 
to an area that is foundational to digital resilience. By sharing population‑level insights de-
rived from real backup and recovery activity, we hope this report helps IT leaders, security 
professionals, and decision‑makers better understand the operational realities behind 
data protection — and make more informed choices as a result.
I hope you enjoy reading it.

Methodology
Data sources and scope
This report is based on aggregated and anonymized query results derived from Keepit’s production backup 
environment between January 1, 2025 and December 31, 2025. In a few cases where explicitly stated, we 
included data for 2024 as well. The dataset reflects backup and restore activity across Keepit customers 
and includes data from all active data center regions: Denmark, Germany, Switzerland, United Kingdom, 
Canada, USA, and Australia. The analysis focuses on population‑level patterns and trends rather than orga-
nization‑specific behavior. At no point were individual customers, users, or datasets identifiable within the 
analytical process.
Data extraction and anonymization
To ensure strict separation between operational systems and analytical work, the dataset was extracted 
internally and anonymized prior to analysis. Data preparation was conducted by designated internal stake-
holders, who transferred the anonymized dataset to a dedicated server environment intended exclusively 
for analytical purposes. This approach ensured that analysts operated only on de‑risked data and had no 
access to live systems or customer‑identifiable information.
Data preparation and indexing
Before analysis commenced, the anonymized dataset was ingested and indexed to support efficient que-
rying and aggregation. Aggregated tables were created to enable analysis at scale while minimizing reli-
ance on granular operational records. This preparation improved query performance, ensured consistency 
across analytical outputs, and reduced the risk of exposing sensitive operational detail while preserving 
analytical validity.
Analytical approach
The analysis examined structural characteristics and recurring patterns related to backup and restore activ-
ity, including backup volumes, restore frequency and size, failure occurrences, change activity, and access 
behavior. Queries were executed against the aggregated dataset to surface distributions and trends ob-
servable across the full dataset. Results were interpreted at an aggregate level suitable for external report-
ing, with emphasis on comparability and clarity.
Validation and analytical review
Analytical outputs were reviewed iteratively to assess internal consistency, validate assumptions, and de-
termine relevance for publication. Findings were discussed between internal stakeholders and the external 
analysts to ensure that interpretations were supported by the data and that methodological constraints 
were appropriately reflected. Results that were insufficiently robust, overly granular, or ambiguous were 
excluded or further refined prior to inclusion.
Reporting and synthesis
Final results were synthesized and contextualized for reporting by the report author, with validation input 
from the external analyst. All findings are presented in aggregated form and are intended to illustrate 
general trends and behaviors across the dataset rather than precise measurements of individual customer 
environments.
Methodological limitations
The findings presented in this report are subject to several limitations. The use of anonymized and aggre-
gated data restricts the ability to account for contextual variables such as organizational size, industry, or 
IT maturity. The analysis reflects the structure and availability of operational data at the time of extraction; 
changes to underlying data models may affect comparability over time. In addition, aggregation may ob-
scure low‑frequency or edge‑case events that could be significant at an individual organizational level. 
These limitations should be considered when interpreting the results.

Data set observations
This chapter examines structural characteristics of backed-up datasets. It analyzes back­
up size development, file and byte change ratios, and regional variation, highlighting how 
data grows, changes, and behaves over time across different environments.
Backup size
When an organization first starts using Keepit Backup and Recovery, the first backup of the entire dataset is 
a major one. In relative terms, if the initial backup is 100%, subsequent backups each pull an additional 2% 
of data each.
There are exceptions: occasional spikes for some customers. This is typically caused by major organiza-
tional changes — i.e., mergers, acquisitions, or other operations that add significant datasets to the existing 
backup.

Chapter 3
User behavior
This section examines how organizations use backup and recovery in practice, including 
how often data is restored, which datasets are accessed, and whether external events 
influence restore activity or testing behavior across organizations, applications, and 
company sizes.
At Keepit, we recommend that organizations regularly test that their backup works, and not only that, but 
that the organization is able to restore critical data to the business environments to ensure business con-
tinuity in the event of data loss or outage events. This is important for business continuity, compliance, and 
brand image.
What we hope to see in this data is that organizations run regular restores — once a year at the bare minimum. 
That would indicate that they perform regular testing.
Restores by organization size
We see a correlation between size of the organization and restore frequency: Small and medium-sized 
businesses (SMBs) are less likely to restore regularly (28% do), while 91% in the commercial segment and 
95% in the enterprise segment are actively conducting and testing restores.

What does that indicate?
The data suggests that restore activity increases with organizational size, which is a natural outcome of hav-
ing more dedicated IT resources, more complex environments, and more frequent operational change. It’s 
also encouraging that most commercial and enterprise organizations show regular restore activity, indicat-
ing that recovery is not just a theoretical capability but something that is exercised in practice.
At the same time, the figures point to an opportunity — especially in the SMB segment — to build more 
routine recovery readiness. Many smaller organizations may experience fewer high-impact incidents day to 
day, and they often operate with limited administrative capacity. In that context, restores can easily become 
an “as-needed” activity rather than a scheduled practice. Introducing lightweight, guided recovery exer-
cises (even simple, repeatable checks) can help SMBs increase confidence, shorten response time when 
incidents occur, and steadily raise operational maturity over time. 
It’s also worth noting that “restore testing” can take multiple forms. While full bulk restore jobs provide the 
strongest validation of end-to-end readiness, even simple file downloads can confirm data accessibility, 
validate that teams know where to find the right data, and reinforce familiarity with recovery tooling. Build-
ing maturity is therefore not only about frequency, but also about progressing from ad hoc retrieval toward 
more structured, guided recovery workflows.

Restores by application/dataset categories
Keepit operates with two categories of applications:  “Productivity applications” and “identity applications.” 
Productivity applications hold the datasets that organizations use to process data and communicate in their 
day-to-day operations — e.g., Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, Salesforce.
Identity applications manage the identities, access rights, rules, permissions, and policies that enable the 
productivity applications to function and comply with the organization’s data regulations — e.g., Microsoft 
Entra ID and Confluence’s Okta.
Because of the nature of identity application data and the way that our statistical material is collected, we’ll 
disregard single file downloads in the following analysis and only consider “real” bulk restore jobs as well as 
bulk downloads.
When we look at how often organizations restore the two categories, we see that, on average, productivity 
applications are restored four times as frequently as identity applications. 
In other words: For every four companies who run a yearly test on their productivity workload, only one of 
them (25%) will have run a test on their identity applications.

What does that indicate?
The difference in restore activity between productivity and identity applications suggests that many orga-
nizations are further along in operationalizing recovery for day-to-day collaboration and business systems 
than they are for identity applications. This is understandable: Productivity datasets are the most visible to 
end users, and restore needs often surface quickly when business content is missing or changed. 
Identity systems, however, have a distinct role in enabling access across the broader SaaS environment. 
Strengthening recovery readiness for identity applications can therefore be seen as the next step in SaaS 
data protection maturity — expanding recovery planning from “business content” to the “access layer” that 
makes business operations possible. As awareness and tooling evolve, guided recovery practices and clear 
runbooks can help make identity recovery testing more approachable and easier to include in regular oper-
ational routines.
Impact on restore activity by external event
To understand if external, high-profile events cause any changes in restore behavior, we looked into two 
events of 2024 and one event of 2025 that could have caused data loss or unavailability. These are events 
we expected would lead IT professionals to double-check their disaster readiness, whether or not their 
organizations were directly impacted by the events. 
We analyzed user behavior around these three events that caused worldwide outages and received exten-
sive media coverage in the IT media. What makes them interesting to analyze is the fact that they generated 
a lot of global  awareness, and that each outage came from a different technological origin. 
1.	 The solar flares of May 2024: solar activity affecting Earth’s magnetic environment.
2.	 The CrowdStrike incident of July 2024: a defective endpoint protection update that crashed Microsoft Windows 
systems.
3.	 The Microsoft outages of October 2025: an Azure control plane misconfiguration causing widespread outages on 
Microsoft 365, Entra ID, and more.
The result: None of these events prompted any change in user behavior. We do not see any sign of increase 
in activity to test that backups were working in the days and weeks following the event.

What does that indicate?
The data shows no measurable increase in restore testing activity following the outage events. One inter-
pretation is that organizations did not experience widespread, immediate restore needs as a direct result 
of these events — an encouraging sign that many environments remained operational despite external 
disruption.
More broadly, the results also suggest that “awareness moments” do not automatically translate into chang-
es in recovery routines. This highlights an opportunity: Organizations can use external events as structured 
triggers for guided recovery checks — short, repeatable validations that reinforce confidence without 
requiring large-scale, disruptive exercises. Over time, this kind of guided approach can help teams build 
steady recovery maturity rather than relying on reactive learning during high-pressure incidents.

Conclusion
Recovery is the true test of backup
The data in this report points to a clear operational takeaway: Backup creates value when organizations 
can recover — confidently, correctly, and efficiently, whether the need is small and immediate or broad and 
time-critical. 
Restore activity is strong among larger organizations. In enterprise and commercial segments, most organi-
zations perform restores regularly, indicating that recovery is not only planned but exercised. This reflects a 
growing level of operational maturity: Many organizations are actively validating that they can regain access 
to data when it matters. 
The same patterns also highlight an important maturity opportunity in the SMB segment. A significant share 
of SMBs did not initiate restores during the year, and bulk restore activity is less common. From an SMB 
perspective, this can be explained by limited administrative bandwidth and the perception that backup is 
something you configure once and rarely touch. The practical implication is not that backup is ineffective, 
but that recovery confidence may be harder to establish without periodic, lightweight testing. Structured, 
guided recovery exercises can help smaller organizations build readiness gradually — without turning re-
covery into a complex project. 
The report also shows that restore behavior is dominated by single file downloads. This reflects real oper-
ational needs: Many incidents are granular, and retrieving a specific file is fast and convenient. At the same 
time, organizations benefit from ensuring they can also execute recovery workflows that scale — where 
larger datasets, multiple users, or entire environments must be restored. Developing proficiency in bulk 
restore is a key step in maturity, turning recovery from an improvised response into a repeatable capability. 
We also observe a consistent execution pattern in bulk restores: Administrators typically restore into a new 
destination, or where files are missing, and overwrite is comparatively rare. This suggests a cautious, risk-
aware approach — favoring clean recovery paths that minimize the chance of unintended changes. This is 
a healthy operational instinct, and it further supports the value of practicing guided restore workflows so 
teams can act quickly while maintaining control. 
Finally, the findings highlight a maturity gap between productivity and identity applications. Identity data is 
restored and tested less frequently than productivity data, even though identity services underpin access 
across many SaaS environments. As organizations expand their SaaS protection strategies, improving iden-
tity recovery readiness is a natural next step — supported by clearer procedures, better visibility, and guided 
recovery assistance. 
Keepit believes AI can help lower the barrier to doing recovery well. Guided recovery — enabled by MCP 
(Model Context Protocol) — opens the door to “asking for help” in the moment that matters. An MCP-en-
abled assistant can help identify unhealthy tenants or suspicious patterns in protected data and guide 
administrators through the right recovery steps, turning recovery into a manageable, repeatable process. 
Even with AI support, regular rehearsal remains valuable — because confidence in recovery is built through 
practice. 
If you would like to discuss what these findings mean for your environment — and how to strengthen maturity 
in bulk restore and guided recovery — please reach out to Keepit to learn more.

Glossary
Job types
Backup
When organizations perform or schedule backups of their data, storing data in the Keepit cloud.
Restore
A restore in Keepit means putting data back into its original environment — repopulating live 
systems with backed‑up data so users can continue working.  Typical use case: operational 
recovery, e.g., restoring a deleted email back into a user’s mailbox.
Bulk restore
A restore operation where more than one file is restored at once, as opposed to a single file 
download.
Single file download
When an administrator locates a single file in a backup set — via browsing or search — and 
downloads it directly through the web interface, either for themselves or to share with an end 
user.
Zip download
Downloading folders of documents as a compressed zip file — retrieving and exporting data 
rather than restoring it to a live system. Typical use case: audits, compliance, or manual review.
PST download
Downloading entire mailboxes as PST files. Typical use case: mailbox archiving, migration, or 
integration into PSBulk restore T‑based business support workflows.

Backup size

Day One
The initial backup of a dataset when an organization first starts using Keepit, serving as the

New files
Files that did not previously exist in the backup dataset and are ingested for the first time.

Existing files that have been modified since the previous backup and are therefore re‑ingested.

The total volume of data ingested from files that are new to the backup dataset. New bytes

up for the first time.
Changed bytes
The total volume of data ingested as a result of changes to existing files. Changed bytes 
represent the size contribution of modifications made to files that already exist in the backup 
dataset.
