Introduction
Identity is now the operational and security control plane of modern business. Platforms like Microsoft 
Entra ID and Okta don’t just manage access: they control how employees work, how systems 
communicate, and how organizations operate securely at scale. 
As identity environments become more interconnected and automated, attackers are increasingly 
targeting Identity Access Management (IAM) systems through ransomware, compromised privileged 
accounts, configuration manipulation, and AI-enhanced phishing and social engineering attacks. 
Yet many organizations still rely primarily on native protections designed for availability, not 
comprehensive identity recovery.
Availability is not recoverability
When identity systems are compromised, misconfigured, or maliciously altered, the impact extends 
far beyond IT. Employees can lose access to critical applications, security policies may fail, automated 
workflows can break, and recovery efforts often become manual, time-consuming, and difficult to 
validate. 
This checklist will help you identify common gaps in identity resilience, recovery readiness, and 
operational protection across your IAM environment. 
1. Are your critical identity systems truly protected?
As identity environments become more interconnected and automated, the impact of a com-
promised admin account, configuration error, or malicious change can spread quickly across 
users, permissions, policies, and business-critical applications. Many organizations still rely 
on manual recovery processes with limited visibility into identity changes, increasing the risk 
of prolonged downtime, security gaps, and operational disruption when incidents occur. 
A strong identity resilience strategy helps reduce this risk through independent, point-in-
time backups stored outside the production environment. Teams can quickly recover users, 
groups, roles, conditional access policies, and MFA configurations without manual effort, 
enabling fast and reliable recovery after ransomware, administrative error, or other identity-
related incidents.
2. Could your organization recover from a compromised identity 
environment? 
Ransomware and identity-based attacks increasingly target privileged accounts, policies, 
and access configurations that organizations depend on to operate securely. As AI-
enhanced phishing, social engineering, and automated attacks become more sophisticated, 
unauthorized changes can spread rapidly across users, groups, permissions, and policies. 
If recovery points are compromised or backups can be altered, organizations may be 
left without a reliable path to clean recovery, increasing the risk of lockouts, operational 
disruption, and costly manual rebuilds.

To strengthen resilience against malicious activity, identity backups should be immutable 
and isolated from the primary IAM environment. Teams should be able to quickly roll back 
unauthorized changes from compromised accounts and rely on tested recovery processes to 
restore identity data, policies, and configurations cleanly and consistently after ransomware 
or other cyber incidents.
3. How quickly could your identity environment recover from disruption?
During a cyber incident or administrative failure, slow and inconsistent recovery processes 
can quickly disrupt employee productivity, application access, and business operations. 
Many organizations still rely on manual scripts, fragmented recovery steps, or undocumented 
processes that make identity restoration difficult to execute under pressure. Limited recovery 
options and unclear recovery time objectives (RTOs) further increase the risk of delays, errors, 
and incomplete restores when rapid recovery matters most. 
Modern identity resilience requires recovery processes that are standardized, tested, and 
aligned to defined recovery time objectives. Teams should be able to quickly restore entire 
environments or recover specific users, groups, roles, and policies with precision, helping 
minimize downtime and maintain operational continuity during identity-related incidents.
4. Could you quickly recover from harmful policy or configuration 
changes?
Identity environments are constantly evolving, with policies, permissions, and access settings 
changing across users, applications, and administrative teams. Without clear visibility into 
historical configurations and the ability to quickly restore known-good settings, even small 
misconfigurations or unauthorized changes can create significant operational and security 
risk. As identity systems become more automated and interconnected, these issues can 
spread quickly, leading to access disruptions, policy drift, and security gaps that are difficult 
to identify and correct. 
Strong configuration and policy resilience helps teams maintain visibility into identity 
changes over time and recover quickly from accidental or malicious modifications. Historical 
versions of policies and settings can be compared, audited, and restored when needed, 
helping reduce operational disruption and maintain a stronger security posture.
5. Could you prove identity recoverability during an audit or investigation? 
As identity systems become more critical to business operations, maintaining clear 
records of identity changes, policy modifications, administrative activity, and recovery 
events is essential for both compliance and incident response. Many organizations still 
lack the visibility and reporting needed to support forensic investigations, demonstrate 
recoverability, or prove compliance with frameworks such as GDPR and SOC 2. During audits 
or security incidents, these gaps can delay response efforts, increase regulatory exposure, 
and create operational and reputational risk. 
Mature identity resilience strategies help reduce this exposure by preserving complete 
audit trails for users, groups, roles, policies, and administrative changes. Backup data can 
be retained in a way that supports legal hold requirements and forensic investigations, 
while recovery and audit reporting helps organizations demonstrate identity protection and 
recovery readiness with greater confidence and transparency.

6. Does your identity recovery strategy extend beyond native IAM 
protections?
Many organizations assume their IAM provider is fully responsible for protecting and 
recovering identity data, policies, and configurations. In reality, native protections are 
primarily designed for service availability, not comprehensive backup and recovery. 
Overreliance on retention policies, recycle bins, or limited rollback capabilities can leave 
critical gaps in identity resilience that often remain hidden until an incident occurs. 
A strong shared responsibility strategy recognizes that identity protection and recoverability 
are essential components of broader cyber resilience planning. Identity systems should be 
treated as Tier 0 infrastructure, with independent backup and recovery capabilities designed 
to protect users, groups, policies, configurations, and other critical identity data from 
operational failure, ransomware, or administrative error.
Now, assess your risk
Identity failures rarely start with a complete outage. More often, they begin with compromised 
accounts, configuration drift, administrative errors, or malicious changes that quietly spread across 
the environment before the impact is fully understood.
